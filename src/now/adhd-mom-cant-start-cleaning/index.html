Your kind of stuck

You do not know where to start

The room has forty things wrong with it and no first one. Your brain is trying to sort all forty before moving, so it does not move.

Your two-minute first step

Pick the corner nearest the door. Only that corner. Two minutes.

Your kind of stuck

It feels too big

You are seeing the whole house as one task. One task that takes a weekend cannot be started on a Tuesday afternoon, so it is not.

Your two-minute first step

Shrink it until it is almost silly: not "the kitchen", but "five dishes into the sink". Then stop or keep going, your call.

Your kind of stuck

You do not have the energy

The will is there and the body is not. After a night of broken sleep, a toddler and a work call, there is nothing left to spend on a bathroom.

Your two-minute first step

Choose the one thing that makes tomorrow morning easier (tonight's dishes, the bag by the door) and give it two minutes sitting down if you need to.

Your kind of stuck

You keep getting pulled away

You start the laundry, see the mail, open the mail, find a form, look for a pen, and twenty minutes later the laundry is still in the machine and three things are half done.

Your two-minute first step

One visible task, one visible timer. Whatever you notice on the way, say "later" out loud and keep walking.

Your kind of stuck

You are waiting on something

You cannot vacuum until the toys are up, cannot put the toys up until the bin is emptied, cannot empty the bin until someone finds the bags. The chain has a missing link and the whole chain stops.

Your two-minute first step

Name the missing link in one sentence. If you can do it in two minutes, that is the task. If you cannot, park the whole thing and pick a room that is not waiting on anyone.

Your kind of stuck

You need a break first

You have been "on" since 6 am. The reason you cannot start the kitchen is not the kitchen.

Your two-minute first step

Set a timer for ten minutes and do nothing useful. Then two minutes on the smallest thing. A rest you planned is not the same as avoiding.

Tuesday, 4:40 pm

A composite from parents we have talked with, not a customer story.

School pickup is done. The small one is asleep in the car seat on the kitchen floor because moving her is a gamble. The kitchen is the kind of mess that has layers: breakfast under lunch under the craft project. Her husband texts "need anything from the shop?" and she wants to answer "a new house".

She stands in the doorway. Her brain offers the whole room at once: the dishes, the floor, the table, the fridge door that will not shut, the bag for the charity shop that has been "going tomorrow" since August. She picks none of them. She picks her phone, which is at least one thing.

What eventually works is not motivation. It is a smaller target. "Put five dishes in the sink." Not the dishes, five dishes. Two minutes on a timer she can see. At the end of the two minutes she has twelve dishes in the sink, the counter half clear, and the strange, familiar feeling that the hard part was over before it began.

That is the whole trick, and it is not a trick. Sari Solden, a therapist who has written about women with ADHD for decades, puts it plainly in her ADDitude piece on overwhelmed moms: running a home means coordinating many loosely defined, repetitive, unrewarded tasks at once, which is exactly the kind of work ADHD makes hardest. The fix is not to try harder at all of it. It is to make the first step so small the room cannot say no.

Three ways to attack a messy house

The weekend blitz

Wait for a free Saturday, do everything. Works for some. For many moms with ADHD the free Saturday never comes, and when it does, the house is so far gone that the blitz takes the whole weekend and the resentment lasts longer.

The system

Zones, rotations, a laminated chart. Good systems help when they are running. The trouble is day one of every system is a starting problem, and that is the thing you came here with.

The two-minute start

One task, one concrete first step, a visible timer, stop allowed. It does not clean the house. It gets you into the room, which is where the house gets cleaned. This is what Now is built around, and what the quiz above hands you.

The National Institute of Mental Health lists trouble organising tasks and difficulty getting started among the inattentive symptoms adults report. Knowing that will not clean the kitchen, but it should take "lazy" off the table. CHADD's adult overview is a fair next read if you want the fuller picture.

Questions moms ask

Why can't I start cleaning even though I want to?

Wanting to and starting are two different jobs in the brain. Starting is an executive function, and executive functions are what ADHD affects most: planning, holding a sequence in mind, and choosing a first action from many. The National Institute of Mental Health lists trouble organising tasks and getting started among adult ADHD symptoms. That is why the problem is the first step, not your character.

Is a messy house a sign of ADHD?

Not on its own. Lots of tidy people have ADHD and lots of messy people do not. A house that stays overwhelming no matter how hard you try, alongside the other patterns on this page, is worth raising with a clinician. This is general information, not medical advice. Talk to your clinician.

What is the fastest way for an ADHD mom to clean a messy house?

There is no fast way, and pages that promise one are selling something. What works is lowering the cost of starting: one room, one concrete step, a short visible timer, and permission to stop. Most people find the second two minutes easier than the first.

Does the Now app clean my house for me?

No. It does one small thing: you type a task, it suggests a first step you can edit, and a two-minute ring counts down. If you tap I'm stuck, it asks what is in the way, with the same six reasons as the quiz above, and gives one next move. It is free, has no account, and does not nag you with streaks.

Should I just hire a cleaner?

If you can afford it, the ADDitude article linked above suggests exactly that, and there is no shame in it. Most of the moms who wrote to us cannot, or the problem is the daily reset between cleaner visits. The quiz is about that daily reset.

Does this quiz diagnose anything?

No. It sorts the way you get stuck into six everyday patterns so you can pick a first step. It is not a screening tool. If you think you might have ADHD, the first step is a conversation with a GP or clinician, and that is a better use of two minutes than any room.
