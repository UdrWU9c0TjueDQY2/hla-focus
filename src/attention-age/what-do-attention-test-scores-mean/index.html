The short answer

An attention test score is a count of how often, how fast and how evenly you responded to a specific set of tasks on a specific day. The words next to the numbers, sustained attention, response control, consistency, distractor handling, object tracking, each name one task family with decades of research behind it. None of them is an age, a percentile or a diagnosis unless the test says exactly where its reference population came from. Below, every term is defined, sourced, and matched to what it looks like inside Attention Age.

How a five-minute attention test works

  1. Three timed tasks. Signal Watch (tap signals, skip decoys), Interference (judge the middle arrow) and Tracking (follow moving shapes): a vigilance task, a flanker task and a multiple-object-tracking task.
  2. Five category scores and one index. Sustained Attention, Response Control, Consistency, Distractor Handling and Object Tracking roll up into a 0–100 Attention Index, each explained in plain language in the app.
  3. A fair re-test. The same tasks and scoring a week later. Changes under 5 points are called "about the same", early gains from familiarity are flagged, and lower results stay in your history too.

Questions people ask about their scores

Is an attention test the same as an ADHD test?

No. Attention tasks like these are used in research and sometimes alongside a clinical assessment, but a score from an app is not a diagnosis of anything. Attention Age says this in its listing: it is an entertainment and self-practice app, and its index is not a medical, clinical or diagnostic test. If you are worried about your attention, talk to a clinician.

Why are my scores different every day?

Sleep, caffeine, time of day, noise and whether you were interrupted all move attention scores. That is why one number means little and a weekly re-test on the same tasks means more.

What is a good Attention Index?

There is no population table behind it, so "good" only means higher than your own earlier sessions under similar conditions. Changes under 5 points are called "about the same" by the app.

Does practicing the drills improve attention in real life?

Practicing a task reliably improves that task. Whether it transfers to work or study is debated in the research and the app does not claim it does; its listing says scores rising with practice shows improvement at the tasks, not proof of broader change.

How long is the test?

About five minutes: three timed tasks. There is also a 20-second practice challenge and a free Daily Signal, one 20-second challenge per day with the same pattern for everyone on that date.

The glossary

Attention span

How long you can keep attention on one thing before it drifts. It is a plain-language idea, not a single measured number, which is why no serious test reports "your attention span is 8 seconds". The famous goldfish comparison has no study behind it; the BBC traced the claim and found no source for it (March 2017).

In Attention Age: Attention Age calls your overall result an Attention Index, 0–100, and says in the app that it is not an age, a percentile or a ranking.

Sustained attention (vigilance)

Staying alert for rare signals over minutes, not seconds. Classic lab versions put you in front of a slow stream of events and ask you to respond to the uncommon one. Performance tends to sag as the minutes pass; see vigilance decrement.

In Attention Age: Measured by Signal Watch: tap the signals, skip the decoys, for the length of the task.

Vigilance decrement

The drop in hit rate and the rise in slow responses that appears a few minutes into a monitoring task. It was first measured in Norman Mackworth's 1948 "clock test" with radar operators (Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology). A test that is only 30 seconds long cannot see it.

In Attention Age: A reason the full test is five minutes rather than one.

Response control (inhibition)

The ability to not respond: holding back on a decoy, stopping a habit mid-flight. Go/no-go tasks measure it by counting commission errors, the times you tapped when you should have waited.

In Attention Age: Scored as the Response Control category. Decoys in Signal Watch are the no-go trials.

Flanker task (interference)

You judge a middle arrow while arrows on either side point the same way (congruent) or the opposite way (incongruent). The slow-down on incongruent trials is the interference cost, described by Eriksen and Eriksen in Perception & Psychophysics, 1974.

In Attention Age: The Interference task: judge the middle arrow. Feeds the Distractor Handling category.

Multiple object tracking

Keeping several moving shapes in mind at once while they cross paths. Most adults can track about four at normal speed; the task was introduced by Pylyshyn and Storm in Spatial Vision, 1988.

In Attention Age: The Tracking task: follow moving shapes. Feeds Object Tracking.

Reaction time (RT)

Milliseconds from a signal appearing to your response. A single fast time means little; the average over many trials means more, and the spread means most.

In Attention Age: Shown per session in your history, not as a public leaderboard.

Consistency (RT variability)

How much your reaction times bounce around trial to trial. Researchers use the standard deviation, or the coefficient of variation (SD divided by the mean) so fast and slow people can be compared fairly. Lapses show up here before they show up in the average.

In Attention Age: The Consistency category. Try the calculator below with your own times.

Baseline

Your first honest result, used only to compare you with yourself later. A baseline taken while tired or interrupted is still a baseline, just a noisy one.

In Attention Age: The first five-minute test. Weekly re-tests use the same tasks and scoring.

Practice effect

Scores on any repeated task rise at first because you learned the task, not because your attention changed. A fair test says so instead of selling the bump as improvement.

In Attention Age: Attention Age flags early gains from familiarity and calls changes under 5 points "about the same".

Validity check

A rule that flags a session that probably was not a real attempt: too many misses, impossible speeds, a long pause. Without it, one distracted session poisons the trend.

In Attention Age: Built into every full test; flagged sessions stay in history but are marked.

Norms and percentiles

Comparing your score with a reference population ("better than 72% of people your age"). Honest norms need a large, documented sample on the same device and conditions. Apps rarely have that, so a percentile from an app is usually decoration.

In Attention Age: Deliberately absent. Your index is compared with your own earlier sessions only.

What your calculator result means

Take the five times you typed above. The mean tells you how fast you were on average; the standard deviation tells you how much you wobbled; the coefficient of variation puts the wobble on a scale that does not depend on raw speed. In attention research, bigger variability goes with more lapses, which is why Attention Age reports Consistency as its own category rather than hiding it inside an average. If one of your five times is far above the others, that single lapse is the number worth noticing.

A short scenario

Tuesday night, 11 pm, after two coffees: five-minute test, index 48, consistency the lowest category. Saturday morning, rested: index 59, consistency up most. Nothing about her brain changed in four days. The conditions did, and the test was honest enough to show it.

The useful habit is not chasing the number. It is re-testing on a similar morning each week and reading the categories, then naming one task and starting a 25-minute session.
